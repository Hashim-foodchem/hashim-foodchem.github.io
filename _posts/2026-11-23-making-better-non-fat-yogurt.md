Yogurt is one of the most widely consumed fermented dairy products. Its creamy texture, pleasant acidity, and nutritional value make it a popular part of many diets. However, producing a high-quality non-fat yogurt is not as simple as removing fat from milk.

Fat contributes to the sensory experience of yogurt, influencing its creaminess, consistency, and mouthfeel. When fat is removed, the final product may become less creamy, excessively firm, or more prone to whey separation.

**Can we improve non-fat yogurt without adding fat?**

This was one of the questions I explored during my doctoral research in food engineering at ITMO University. In a study published in *Foods* in 2021, my colleagues and I investigated how whey protein isolate (WPI) could improve the physical, structural, and sensory properties of non-fat yogurt.

## Where My Curiosity Began: From Whey Beverages to Yogurt Research

My curiosity about whey proteins began during my master's studies in Egypt. I was working with whey retentate and exploring how it could be used to develop a beverage. What interested me most was a practical question: **could a dairy stream that is sometimes undervalued become the basis of a useful, appealing food product?**

Our beverage project achieved **third place** in a competition held at my university. I remember the competition as being organized or supported by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), although I still need to verify the precise organizer and award details before describing them definitively.

That experience encouraged me to look beyond the nutritional content of whey and ask what its proteins could *do* in a food system. Years later, during my PhD, I returned to this curiosity from another direction: could whey protein isolate help improve the texture and structure of non-fat yogurt?

For me, the two projects are connected by the same idea: understanding ingredient functionality can turn an everyday formulation problem into an opportunity for food innovation.

## Why Is Fat Important in Yogurt?

To understand the challenge, we first need to look at yogurt structure.

During fermentation, lactic acid bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid. As the pH decreases, casein proteins gradually aggregate and form a three-dimensional gel network.

This network gives yogurt its characteristic structure and helps retain water.

In full-fat yogurt, fat globules are distributed within this protein network. Depending on the formulation and processing conditions, they contribute to the structure and sensory properties of the final product.

Removing fat changes this balance and may produce several undesirable characteristics:

- Reduced creaminess and a less satisfying mouthfeel.
- Changes in gel firmness and viscosity.
- Increased susceptibility to whey separation, also called *syneresis*.
- Differences in the appearance and overall sensory quality.

The challenge for food scientists is therefore to reproduce desirable textural properties while maintaining a reduced-fat formulation.

## What Makes Whey Proteins Interesting?

Milk contains two major groups of proteins: caseins and whey proteins.

Whey proteins are particularly interesting because their structure changes during heating. When heated sufficiently, proteins such as β-lactoglobulin unfold, exposing groups that can participate in new molecular interactions.

These changes can promote interactions between whey proteins and caseins, contributing to the development of the yogurt gel network.

Whey protein isolate is a concentrated dairy protein ingredient commonly used in food formulation.

Its technological advantages include:

- Water-binding properties.
- Ability to contribute to gel formation.
- Modification of viscosity and texture.
- Potential to improve the structure of reduced-fat products.

Importantly, whey proteins do not literally replace the physical role of fat globules. Instead, they can help compensate for some of the textural changes associated with fat removal.

## What Did We Investigate?

In our published study, we prepared set-style yogurt using skim milk powder and whey protein isolate.

The experimental formulations were standardized to 14% total solids. We replaced different proportions of skim milk powder with WPI at levels of 3%, 5%, 7%, and 9%.

We compared these formulations with full-fat and non-fat control yogurts.

After fermentation, we evaluated several important quality characteristics:

1. **Water-holding capacity:** How effectively the yogurt retains water within its structure.
2. **Texture:** How the yogurt responds to mechanical deformation.
3. **Rheological properties:** How the yogurt flows and deforms when forces are applied.
4. **Microstructure:** How proteins are organized within the gel network.
5. **Sensory properties:** How the different formulations compare in appearance, texture, and flavor.

By combining these measurements, we could better understand not only whether WPI improved yogurt quality, but also how these changes were related to the food's internal structure.

## What Did We Find?

### 1. Better Water Retention

One important observation was that increasing WPI substitution improved the water-holding capacity of non-fat yogurt.

For example, water-holding capacity increased from approximately 44.8% in the non-fat control to 50.6% at the highest WPI substitution level.

This suggests that incorporating whey proteins helped the yogurt structure retain more water.

Better water retention is important because visible whey separation can negatively influence consumer perceptions of product quality.

### 2. Changes in Texture and Flow

The addition of WPI also influenced yogurt consistency.

In our experiments, higher WPI substitution generally increased apparent viscosity while reducing the firmness measured by the texture analyzer.

These findings highlight an important distinction: **a thicker yogurt is not necessarily a firmer yogurt.**

Viscosity describes resistance to flow, whereas firmness reflects resistance to a particular mechanical deformation. Both properties contribute to the eating experience, but they measure different aspects of texture.

### 3. Changes in Microstructure

Microscopic observations revealed differences between the protein networks of control and WPI-fortified yogurts.

The WPI-containing samples generally exhibited a more compact protein network than the non-fat control.

Such structural changes can help explain differences in water retention and mechanical behavior.

### 4. Consumer Acceptance Requires Balance

More protein does not automatically mean better yogurt.

Although WPI improved several functional properties, the highest substitution level was not the most favorable in sensory evaluation.

The 3% and 5% WPI formulations achieved relatively favorable overall sensory scores, while higher substitution levels were less acceptable.

This demonstrates why optimizing a food formulation requires balancing instrumental measurements with sensory quality.

## What Does This Mean for Food Product Development?

Our findings support the use of whey protein isolate as a promising ingredient for improving non-fat yogurt.

However, successful formulation requires more than increasing protein concentration.

Food developers need to consider protein composition, heat treatment, fermentation conditions, structural interactions, and consumer preferences.

It is also important to recognize that our findings were obtained under specific laboratory conditions using set-style yogurt. Additional studies would be needed to assess performance across other formulations, processing systems, and storage conditions.

For me, this research illustrates a broader principle in food science: **understanding the relationship between ingredient functionality, microstructure, and sensory properties is essential for designing better foods.**

## Key Takeaways

- Removing fat changes yogurt texture and structure.
- Whey protein isolate can improve water retention and modify yogurt consistency.
- Protein interactions during heating and fermentation contribute to gel development.
- Viscosity, firmness, and sensory quality do not necessarily change in the same direction.
- Optimal formulation requires balancing technological functionality with consumer acceptance.
